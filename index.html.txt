    <h2>Dave’s Story</h2>
    <p>In September 2023, Dave suffered a life-threatening aortic aneurysm that cut off blood flow to his kidneys. He survived the emergency surgery, but the aneurysm returned larger than before.</p>
    <p>On April 25, 2025, he underwent a second major open-heart surgery. The operation brought serious complications. He spent three and a half weeks in the hospital before finally being discharged on May 28.</p>
    <p>A stent that had been placed was destroying his red blood cells (hemolysis). He was not strong enough for further reconstructive surgery. Throughout June he required blood transfusions nearly every Tuesday and Thursday just to stay stable.</p>
    <p>In July the medical team developed a plan to address the stent and discovered multiple endoleaks. Over the following seven months Dave endured repeated procedures to seal those leaks — each one requiring at least three days in the ICU.</p>
    <p>On June 3, 2026, after this long and difficult journey, Dave was finally declared healthy enough to be evaluated for a kidney transplant. Then in July he was diagnosed with congestive heart failure. After careful management with the heart-failure clinic, he received clearance this week to begin the search for a living kidney donor.</p>
    <div class="highlight">
      <strong>Today Dave is ready.</strong> He has fought through years of surgeries, complications, and setbacks. A living kidney donor would give him the chance at a healthier future.
    </div>

  <section>
    <h2>Why a Living Donor Matters</h2>
    <div class="facts">
      <div class="fact">
        <strong>Better outcomes</strong> — Living-donor kidneys typically last longer and begin working immediately compared with deceased-donor kidneys.
      </div>
      <div class="fact">
        <strong>Shorter wait</strong> — Finding a living donor can mean receiving a transplant months or years sooner than waiting on the deceased-donor list.
      </div>
      <div class="fact">
        <strong>You don’t have to be a perfect match</strong> — Paired exchange programs (including those used by Yale) allow incompatible donors to still help by swapping with other pairs.
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>
